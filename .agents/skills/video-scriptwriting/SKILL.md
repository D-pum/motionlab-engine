# Video Scriptwriting

Use the brief, approved title or thumbnail promise, research, and any available scripts the creator has approved. Match the creator's voice, pacing, and handoff format when there are examples. If examples are unavailable, use conversational narration and state any assumption about format or runtime that affects the draft. Preserve approved wording and untouched sections during targeted revisions.

## Develop the script

- Define what the viewer came to learn or see and what the ending delivers. Open with a specific question, striking detail, action, or visual that starts fulfilling the promise. Choose the opening that fits the subject.
- Build beats around what the viewer needs to understand next. Each beat should advance the story, explain a cause, resolve a question, or change what the viewer sees. Use transitions that carry the thought forward. Deliver the payoff where it has the most impact.
- Write for the ear. Read the narration aloud or rehearse it silently at speaking pace, then trim repeated ideas, awkward phrasing, and sentences that overload a single breath. Treat runtime as an estimate until a read-through confirms it. Leave room for pauses and moments that rely on visuals or sound.
- Verify consequential claims, numbers, quotations, and chronology against available reliable sources. Keep citations and uncertainty in production notes. Flag unresolved facts that could change the narration and avoid invented dialogue or evidence.

## Hand off to production

Use the project's existing format when available. For a full script without an established format, give each beat a heading with an optional estimated time range, followed by exact spoken lines and any relevant visual, on-screen text, or sound cues. Clearly distinguish voiceover from directions so narration can be copied into a recording or text-to-speech tool. Describe what an editor needs to show and call out when a cue requires footage or assets whose availability is uncertain.

For Shorts, establish the premise quickly and focus the script on one central development and payoff. For longer videos, give each section a clear purpose and use only as much setup as the viewer needs. ## Hooks, engagement, and calls to action

For MotionLab viral and promotional openings, default to the creator's **context → lean → snap** approach when it fits:

1. **Context** establishes the subject or situation immediately.
2. **Lean** steers the viewer toward a specific, plausible expectation.
3. **Snap** overturns the expectation created by the lean and reveals the real angle. Carry that reveal into the next beat.

The InexWorks promo opens on a building, leads the viewer to think about its height, then pivots to the finish as the part people remember. On review, identify the expectation the lean sets up and the exact line or image that overturns it.

- Make the first spoken line and first visual work together when both are specified. Establish a clear reason to keep watching and make sure the script delivers what the hook promises.
- Sustain interest through meaningful reveals, concrete examples, visual changes that help explain the story, or questions the video answers. Where a section drags, improve the beat or trim it.
- When the brief or channel style calls for a CTA, choose one primary action that fits the goal. For comments, ask a specific question viewers can answer from their experience or what they just watched. For a follow, subscription, or next video, give a concrete reason to act. Place the CTA after a useful moment or at a natural transition and write it as a spoken line. Skip it when the format or ending works better without one.

Before handing off, check that any CTA fits the video and preserves the payoff, the opening matches the video's promise, the ending pays it off, the narration fits the target duration, and any essential visual cues are actionable. Deliver the requested stage, such as an outline, a revised section, or a recording-ready script.
